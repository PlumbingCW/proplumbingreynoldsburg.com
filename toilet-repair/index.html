Toilet repair in Reynoldsburg, OH covers the parts inside the tank, the seal under the bowl, the supply line and the drain below. Installation means setting a new bowl on a sound flange so it stays tight.

Act first

When an overflowing toilet cannot wait

A bowl that climbs and does not drop usually means a stoppage in the trap or the branch line. Pouring more water or flushing again makes the floor wet fast. Call after hours if the water is reaching the rim, or if more than one drain in the house is slow, since that points past the toilet.

  • Shut the supply valve behind the bowl by turning it clockwise.
  • Lift the tank lid and hold the flapper closed.
  • Throw towels at the base and open a window if it smells.
  • Do not run the shower or washer while the line is blocked.
  • Skip chemical drain cleaners, they can burn you when a plunger splashes back.
What to look for

Why your toilet keeps running

A toilet that hisses or refills on its own is almost always one of these.

Worn flapper

The rubber disk no longer seals the tank outlet. Water seeps into the bowl and the fill valve tops the tank up every few minutes.

Fill valve stuck open

Grit or a cracked seal keeps water flowing after the float rises. You will hear a steady hiss.

Float set too high

Water climbs past the overflow tube and drains down it. Lowering the float often stops this.

Chain too tight

A short chain holds the flapper slightly open. A bit of slack lets it drop fully.

Hard to tell

Drop a few drops of food coloring in the tank. Color in the bowl without flushing means the flapper leaks.

Scale, hardness and the fill valve

Columbus softens its water to an average of 120 parts per million, about 7 grains per gallon, and Reynoldsburg is listed as a Columbus water and sewer contract community. That is moderate water, so rubber flappers and fill valve seals still collect mineral film over the years. If your tank parts feel slick or chalky when you lift them, plan on replacing the set instead of cleaning it again.

Side by side

Fix the toilet you have or replace it

The answer depends on the bowl, not just the symptom. After-hours calls get the same diagnosis, but we often suggest a temporary shutoff and a daytime swap.

Repair makes sense when

  • The porcelain is sound with no hairline cracks.
  • Only tank parts or the supply line are failing.
  • The bowl flushes strongly and does not clog often.
  • The base is dry and the toilet does not rock.

Replacement makes sense when

  • The tank or bowl is cracked and weeping.
  • It clogs weekly even with a clear drain line.
  • Parts for an old model are hard to source.
  • You want a taller bowl or a better flush.

Leak at the base: wax ring and flange

A toilet sits on a wax or foam ring that seals the bowl to the floor flange. When the toilet rocks, the ring compresses unevenly and begins to seep, first a damp spot, later a soft floor or a stain on the ceiling below. We pull the bowl, check the flange for cracks or a flange that sits below the finished floor, and reset the toilet on a new seal.

A broken flange is the part most people miss. A new wax ring on a cracked flange leaks again within months, so we repair or replace the flange when it is damaged. Caulk around the bowl does not stop a leak. It only hides it. If the source is unclear, a leak tracing visit separates a bad seal from a supply drip.

How it works

How a toilet installation goes

Setting a new toilet is mostly about the floor and flange, not the bowl, and it follows the same rules as other fixture installation work. Same-day appointments are available when the schedule allows.

1

Shut and drain

Water off, tank flushed, remaining water sponged out of the bowl.

2

Pull the old unit

We lift it straight up and stuff a rag in the flange to stop sewer gas.

3

Inspect the flange

We look for rust, cracks and height before anything new goes down.

4

Set and level

New seal, bolts snug in turns so porcelain does not crack, shims where the floor dips.

5

Connect and test

New supply line, several flushes, and a dry-paper check around the base.

Checklist

Two minutes of checking first

A few minutes of looking saves a trip.

  • Press on the tank and check for a hairline crack.
  • Rock the bowl side to side, note any movement.
  • Dry the supply line and valve, then look for beads.
  • See whether other drains gurgle when you flush.
  • Turn the supply valve and confirm it still closes.
