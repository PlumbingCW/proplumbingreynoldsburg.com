A water softener swaps hardness minerals for sodium or potassium inside a resin tank, and a filter removes particles, chlorine taste or odor. In Reynoldsburg, OH, many homeowners ask whether they need either one.

The honest answer is that it depends on what bothers you. Columbus softens its water before delivery, so your starting water is already moderate. We install and repair these systems, and we will tell you if you can skip one.

Good to know

Columbus already softens the water you buy

According to the Columbus Division of Water, Columbus softens its water on average to 120 parts per million, or approximately 7 grains per gallon. Columbus lists Reynoldsburg as a water and sewer contract community. Read the Water for Living Consumer Confidence Report for current figures. So a home softener goes further than the city, and is a choice about spots, soap feel and scale, not an emergency.

Why some homes still want one

Moderate water still leaves scale on faucets, a film on shower glass and white crust inside kettles. Water at roughly 7 grains still builds slowly inside a tank heater or a tankless unit. Some people also dislike how soap feels in moderately hard water, and a softener changes that.

Others find the maintenance, the salt and the extra sodium not worth it. That is a fair call. A softener adds a tank, a drain connection and a monthly chore, and it uses some water to regenerate. We would rather explain the tradeoffs than sell one.

Side by side

Softener or carbon filter

These solve different problems, and homeowners often mix them up.

Softener fits when

  • You see scale on fixtures and glass
  • You want softer feeling water at every tap
  • You accept salt refills and a drain line
  • You protect a tank or tankless heater from scale

Filter fits when

  • You dislike chlorine taste or smell
  • You want sediment removed
  • You want no salt and little upkeep
  • You want one tap or the whole house treated

How it works

What a softener install involves

A softener is plumbing work, and the layout matters.

1

Pick the spot

The unit needs space, power, a drain and a place near the water entry.

2

Plan the bypass

A bypass valve lets you isolate the unit for service without shutting off water.

3

Keep outdoor taps raw

We usually leave outdoor spigots and some cold lines untreated.

4

Connect and flush

Piping goes in, the drain is set with an air gap and the media is flushed.

5

Program it

We set the regeneration for your household use and walk you through the salt.

Repairing a water conditioner

Softeners fail in a few ways. A salt bridge forms a crust inside the brine tank and keeps salt from dissolving. A clogged injector or a worn valve stops regeneration. A resin bed that has worn out lets hardness through no matter how much salt you add.

If the water has gone hard, the first check is whether the tank has salt and whether it regenerates. We also check the bypass valve and the drain line. A broken valve or a tired resin bed is a repair decision, and for an old unit it can lead to replacement instead.

Checklist

Before you call about softener trouble

Check these first, since several are easy fixes.

  • Confirm the unit has power and a lit display
  • Look for salt, and poke the pile for a crust
  • Check that the bypass valve is not left open
  • Note the date it last regenerated
  • Watch the drain line for a steady trickle
  • Test a tap for soap feel and spotting
Cost factors

What affects softener and filter cost

The system and the plumbing layout drive the price.

Type of system

A softener, a carbon filter and a combined setup each use different equipment.

Capacity

Larger households need larger resin tanks and more frequent regeneration.

Plumbing changes

Tight spaces, long runs and drain access change the install labor.

Existing equipment

A repair on a working unit is smaller than a new install.

Water treatment equipment is generally a home improvement and not an insured repair, so coverage is rare. Ask your insurer or home warranty provider if you are unsure.
