Leak detection is the work of locating a water loss you cannot see, using meter readings, pressure tests, acoustic listening and thermal imaging, so repairs open the smallest possible spot. Active water loss is urgent even when it is slow. Our 24/7 emergency dispatch covers it, and if a pipe is spraying, close the main shutoff first and then call.

The bill jumped, the wall is stained, and nothing is dripping

Most hidden leaks announce themselves sideways. The water bill creeps up for two cycles. A baseboard swells. The basement smells like wet cardboard on a dry week. A faucet is not dripping anywhere, so people assume it is the weather or a different problem.

The technical name for the work that sorts this out is leak detection. It is a process of elimination: prove there is a leak, decide which part of the system it is in, then narrow it down to a few feet. That order saves you from a hole in the wrong wall.

How it works

Check the meter and your high bill first

A water meter is the cheapest leak detector you own. This takes ten minutes and nothing but a flashlight.

1

Stop all water

Shut off taps, the ice maker and the washer. Make sure no one flushes or runs the softener regeneration.

2

Read the meter

Open the lid and look for the small triangle or dial that spins at low flow. If it moves with everything off, water is leaving somewhere.

3

Write the numbers

Note the register digits, wait an hour with nothing running, and read again. Any change is a loss, and the size of it hints at the leak size.

4

Split the house

Close the main shutoff inside the house. If the meter still moves, the leak is between the meter and your house. If it stops, the loss is inside.

5

Test the toilets

A few drops of food coloring in each tank, no flushing for fifteen minutes. Color in the bowl means a flapper is leaking.

Why a quiet leak raises the water bill

A pinhole at normal pressure can pass a few gallons an hour. That is under a dribble, so you see nothing, but over a billing period it adds up to thousands of gallons. A running toilet flapper does the same thing and is the most common culprit we see.

If the meter test shows steady loss, ask your water utility how your meter is read and how bills are estimated. A bill that jumps after an estimated reading is a different problem than a real leak, and the meter test tells you which one you have.

Ceiling and wall leaks

A brown ring on a ceiling usually sits below the real source, because water follows framing and pipe sleeves before it drips. We start with the room above: a tub overflow, a failed supply connector, a shower pan or a drain joint. Wall stains behind a vanity or a washer box often trace to a supply line crimp or a loose fitting.

Thermal imaging and a moisture meter show the wet area without cutting. We then pressure test the suspect line to confirm. The aim is one small access hole, sized to the pipe, not a rolled-up section of drywall.

Checklist

Signs of a hidden leak before the stain shows

These clues point to water you cannot see.

  • Bill rises with no change in how many people live there
  • Water meter dial moves when every fixture is off
  • Hissing or trickling sound inside a quiet wall
  • Paint bubbling or wallpaper seams lifting at one spot
  • Musty smell that returns after you air out the room
  • Floor boards cupping or vinyl seams separating
  • Warm spot on a floor over a hot water line

Under-house and crawlspace leaks

In a crawlspace you can see what a slab hides. Supply lines hang from joists, drain lines sag between hangers, and a small drip leaves a dark trail on subfloor wood or a patch of damp insulation. Look for rusted hangers, mold on the underside of the floor, and a chronic smell.

We separate supply from drain: a supply leak drips under pressure all day, while a drain leak only runs when someone uses a fixture. That one test tells us which line to isolate. Wear gloves and a respirator down there, and stay out if you see standing water near wiring.

Buried water line leaks outside the house

The service line runs from the meter to the house underground. A leak there may show as a soggy patch in a dry lawn, a sinkhole, low pressure, or a meter that spins with the house shut off. The meter split test above separates it from interior problems.

We listen along the route with an acoustic microphone, because water escaping a pressurized pipe makes a distinct hiss in the soil. Digging is the last step. In Ohio the person digging calls OHIO811 first, and that is a minimum of 2 working days before the shovel, so plan the repair around it. Our water line repair work picks up where detection stops.

Slab leaks under the floor

A slab leak is a leak in a pipe running under or inside a concrete floor, common on homes built on a slab and with copper lines in the concrete. Signs: a warm or hot spot on the floor, the sound of running water at night, carpet damp from the underside, or a bill that rises with no visible source.

We confirm with a pressure test and then pinpoint with the acoustic microphone. Your options are repairing at the leak point by opening the slab, rerouting the line through walls or attic, or repiping. Rerouting avoids breaking concrete but opens walls. We explain the tradeoffs and our whole-house repipe work when the pipe itself is the problem.

Act first

When a water leak cannot wait until morning

If water is running onto a floor, through a ceiling, or around electrical outlets, treat it as an emergency. Call us for 24/7 dispatch, and do these things while we are on the way.

  • Close the main water valve at the meter or heater
  • Turn off breakers for any wet room if you can reach them dry
  • Open a low faucet to drain the pressure out of the lines
  • Move furniture and electronics away from the wet area
  • Photograph the damage for your records and insurer

Leak you can chase yourself versus leak to call in

Some water problems are a ten minute fix. Others need gear and a licensed hand.

Reasonable to try yourself

  • Dye test for a running toilet tank
  • Tightening a loose supply connector by hand
  • Reading the meter with all water off
  • Checking visible pipes under sinks with a dry tissue

Time to stop and call

  • Meter moves and you cannot see any drip
  • Warm floor, hissing wall, or sagging ceiling
  • Water near panels, outlets or ceiling light fixtures
  • Any digging near a buried line or utility

Irrigation, pool and yard leaks

Irrigation: a zone that stays wet, a valve that ticks, or a lawn patch that is greener than the rest points to a lateral line or a stuck valve. Shut the system off and watch the meter. If it stops, the leak is in the sprinkler loop.

Pool and spa: water level that drops faster than normal evaporation can mean a plumbing leak in the return lines. A bucket test, with the bucket on a step and water levels marked inside and outside, separates evaporation from a loss. We cover the plumbing lines but not pool liners or equipment.

Yard and meter box: a meter box full of water, or a wet ring around the curb stop, can mean a leak at the meter couplings. That part may belong to the utility, so ask your water utility who owns it before anyone digs. In Reynoldsburg, Blacklick Creek can keep ground damp after rain, so a wet yard alone is not proof. A meter that runs with the house off is.

What drives the cost of finding a leak

You hear the price first. These factors change how long detection takes.

How hidden it is

An exposed supply line takes minutes. A line under a slab takes listening gear and patient work.

Number of tests

Isolating zones, pressure testing and moisture mapping each add time, but narrow the opening.

Access to the area

Finished basements, tight crawlspaces and attics slow the work.

Repair that follows

Locating is one job. Repairing the pipe, patching drywall and restoring floors are separate.

Hour of the call

Night calls and daytime appointments are priced separately.
