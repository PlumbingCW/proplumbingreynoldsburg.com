When the creek rises, the basement follows

Gahanna began as a settlement on Big Walnut Creek in 1849, and the creek system still decides how wet a lot feels. Rocky Fork Creek and Sycamore Run both run into it. After days of rain, groundwater presses on foundation walls and a sump pump becomes the only barrier between a dry basement and a soaked one.

A pump that has sat idle for months fails in three common ways: a stuck float, a dead motor or a clogged discharge line. We test all three. For a failing unit, sump pump repair is usually quicker than replacement, though an old pump with a burned motor is better swapped. If seepage keeps returning through the walls, basement waterproofing deals with the cause, not just the symptom.

Pump trouble: repair or replace

The choice depends on what failed and how much work the pit has already done.

Repair makes sense when

  • The float sticks but the motor hums
  • The discharge line is only partly clogged
  • The check valve is chattering or stuck
  • The pump is newer and the pit is clean

Replace makes sense when

  • The motor smells burned or trips the breaker
  • The casing is cracked or heavily corroded
  • It cycles constantly even in dry weather
  • You have already had repeated failures

Before we arrive

Before We Arrive: Protecting a Wet Lower Level

Flooding changes the order of operations, so power comes before cleanup.

  1. Main valve first

    If a supply pipe is leaking, turn the main shutoff clockwise. If rainwater is the source, closing the main will not stop it, so move to the next step.

  2. Basement breakers off

    Switch off lower-level circuits from a dry spot. If the panel itself is wet, stay out and call your electric utility.

  3. Lift what you can

    Raise boxes, paper and small appliances onto shelves or upper floors before the water reaches them.

  4. Phone the 24/7 line

    Tell us whether water comes from the pit, a drain or a pipe, and we will tell you what to watch for.
