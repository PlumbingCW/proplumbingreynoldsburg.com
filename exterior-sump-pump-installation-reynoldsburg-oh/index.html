Exterior Sump Pump Installation in Reynoldsburg, OH

Where the water leaves your house matters as much as the pump. Here is how the outdoor side of a sump system is planned.

By the Reynoldsburg Pro Plumbing teamOctober 16, 2026Reynoldsburg, OH

An exterior sump pump installation moves groundwater from a pit inside the basement to a discharge point outside, and the outdoor run is where most mistakes happen. In Reynoldsburg, OH, a good layout sends water well away from the foundation, keeps the line from freezing shut, and never drains onto a neighbor's lawn. This guide covers each piece in the order a plumber plans it.

What the exterior side of the system actually includes

People say exterior pump when they mean the discharge side: the pipe that leaves the house through the rim joist, the fitting where it exits, the buried or surface run, and the outlet that spreads the water. The pump itself still sits in the pit indoors.

Some homes also have a second pit or a French drain outside the footing that feeds the main pit. Each piece has a job, and the weakest link decides how long the system works during a heavy storm.

How it works

How a discharge line gets planned and built

The order matters because each step sets up the next one.

1

Pick the outlet

Find a spot at least ten feet from the foundation on ground that slopes away. Avoid driveways, shared fences, and the street.

2

Call before digging

Request a line locate before any trench is opened. Wait for the utilities to mark what they own.

3

Core the wall

Drill a clean hole through the rim joist or foundation wall above the pit. Seal around the pipe so water and pests stay out.

4

Run and slope

Lay rigid pipe with a steady fall toward the outlet. Add a check valve near the pump so water does not run back.

5

Finish the outlet

Fit a pop-up emitter or a screened end that opens under pressure and sheds ice or debris.

Side by side

Buried line or surface line: the tradeoffs

Both can work, but they fail in different ways.

Buried discharge

  • Looks cleaner and stays out of the mower's way
  • Needs a trench and a locate before digging
  • Can freeze if water sits in a flat section
  • Repair means digging up the pipe

Surface discharge

  • Faster to install and easy to inspect
  • Can be tripped over or damaged by tools
  • Flexible hose may kink under snow
  • Exposed pipe cools quickly in cold snaps

Freeze and clog protection for winter

A discharge pipe that holds water through a freeze can plug with ice, and then the pit fills during the next thaw. Slope the pipe so it empties completely after each cycle. Keep the outlet clear of leaves and mulch.

If the line has a flat spot or a belly, expect trouble in January. A bypass outlet higher up on the pipe, sometimes called a relief port, gives water a second route when the end is blocked.

Act first

Signs the system is already failing

If your pit is full and the pump is silent, act before the next storm. Standing water in a basement damages drywall, stored items, and electrical equipment.

  • Unplug the pump and check the outlet for a plug or ice
  • Test the float switch by lifting it by hand
  • Do not stand in water if outlets or a panel are nearby
  • Shut off basement power at the panel if water reaches the outlets
  • Call a plumber for pump replacement or discharge repair

What changes the scope of the job

Four things shape the work: how far the discharge run is, whether the yard needs digging or a surface line works, whether a new core hole is required, and whether the pit needs replacement or a battery backup. A plumber measures these on site.

For homes with a pump that is already running but struggling, our sump pump repair visits check the float, the check valve, and the discharge before recommending a new unit. When the real cause is groundwater around the footing, we may point you toward basement waterproofing instead.

Discharge problems do not stop at the municipal border. From our Reynoldsburg base, we dispatch crews to Pickerington and Gahanna, where a lot's slope and the neighbor's yard often decide where the pipe can safely end.

Local note

Reynoldsburg note: dig notification comes first

Ohio law requires whoever digs to notify OHIO811 before breaking ground. Dial 811, give at least 2 working days of notice, and no more than 16 calendar days ahead. Utilities mark the lines they own, so a trench for a sump discharge should not start until those flags are up.

Privately owned lines, such as some service lines, may not be marked, and OHIO811 says those are the homeowner's responsibility. A private locator may be needed. The contractor doing the work makes the notification, so confirm who is placing the call before the schedule is set.

Source type: OHIO811 homeowner information page (oups.org).
